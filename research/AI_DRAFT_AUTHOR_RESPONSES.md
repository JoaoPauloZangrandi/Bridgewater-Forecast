## 1. Central Thesis and Causal Mechanism

I do not think AI and modern mercantilism are separate trends that happen to
coincide. I think they form a single feedback system. AI makes some forms of
cognition cheaper and more abundant, but it does so by increasing demand for
inputs that remain slow, local, regulated, and politically contestable:
electricity, advanced chips, mineral refining, data-center sites, engineering
talent, capital, and access to trusted jurisdictions. The decisive economic
scarcity therefore moves away from intelligence itself and toward assured
access to the complements required to deploy intelligence at scale.

That shift changes both corporate strategy and state behavior. Firms cannot
treat the cheapest global supplier as reliable when export controls, sanctions,
tariffs, grid queues, or diplomatic alignment can interrupt access. Governments
cannot treat leading-edge compute or critical minerals as ordinary traded
goods when those inputs influence military capability, industrial productivity,
and bargaining power. Both therefore pay an insurance premium for redundancy:
long-term power contracts, domestic fabrication, subsidized refining, sovereign
compute, strategic inventories, and preferential trade relationships. What
looks inefficient in a frictionless model can be rational once interruption
risk is priced.

The mechanism is circular. Better AI raises expected demand for compute and
power. That demand increases the rents attached to physical and institutional
chokepoints. Higher rents and greater security concerns lead states to subsidize
domestic capacity, restrict strategic exports, and favor aligned suppliers.
Those policies duplicate fixed costs and fragment markets, raising the value of
scale, financing, and political access. The resulting race for capacity then
supports further AI investment. The loop can produce extraordinary capital
formation before it produces equally extraordinary measured productivity,
because construction is faster than organizational redesign and because part
of the investment buys resilience rather than output.

This is not a forecast of complete deglobalization. Most trade should persist
because specialization remains valuable. Fragmentation should concentrate at
the strategic nodes of otherwise global networks: advanced semiconductors,
cloud infrastructure, energy systems, critical-mineral processing, defense
inputs, and selected technologies. Countries will continue trading broadly
while discriminating sharply at those nodes.

My base case is therefore a bottlenecked AI boom: capabilities and adoption
advance quickly, physical investment remains elevated, aggregate productivity
improves only gradually, and trade is reorganized into overlapping preferential
lanes rather than cleanly divided blocs. The framework would be wrong if rapid
algorithmic efficiency and substitution made physical inputs abundant, if
industrial policy repeatedly failed without being renewed, and if major powers
restored nondiscriminatory trade despite continuing strategic rivalry.

### F11: The WTO Appellate Body remains below quorum (82%)

This forecast rests on institutional path dependence rather than a precise
macro scenario. The underlying disagreement concerns the authority of appellate

### F01: METR reports at least a one-week task horizon (75%)

The relevant capability measure has advanced much faster than ordinary economic
diffusion. A one-week threshold by the end of 2029 does not require artificial

specified task-completion frontier under METR's methodology. I retain a 25%
failure probability because extrapolating a short trend is dangerous, task
horizons may saturate, and evaluation methodology can change as agents become
more capable.

## 4. Strongest Argument Against the Framework

The strongest counterargument is that the framework mistakes a temporary
hardware build-out and a temporary protectionist cycle for durable structural
scarcity. In this alternative, algorithmic efficiency, smaller models,
open-weight competition, specialized chips, and higher utilization reduce the
compute and electricity required for each useful unit of intelligence faster
than demand grows. AI then diffuses through existing devices and cloud capacity
without requiring the projected physical build-out. At the same time, tariffs
and localization policies prove expensive and politically fragile. Firms route
around them, fiscal constraints weaken subsidies, new refining technologies
reduce incumbency advantages, and governments retreat after consumers bear
higher prices. AI remains transformative, but it becomes less resource-intensive
just as mercantilism loses political support. The two forces decouple rather
than reinforce one another.

## 5. Two Candidate Passages for the Final Framework

### Passage A: The Assured-Access Premium

The price that matters in the next decade will not always be the spot price of
an input. It will be the price of being confident that the input is available
when a firm or state needs it. A cheaper chip exposed to export controls, a
cheaper mineral refined through a strategic rival, or a cheaper data-center
site waiting years for grid access can have a higher risk-adjusted cost than a
nominally expensive domestic alternative. This creates an assured-access
premium: the wedge decision-makers will pay for reliability across political,
physical, and contractual states of the world. The premium explains why
apparently inefficient duplication can persist. A second fab, grid connection,
supplier, or trade route does not need to beat the incumbent on ordinary unit
cost. It needs to be valuable in the states where the incumbent is unavailable.
Industrial policy amplifies this logic because governments absorb part of the
insurance cost while firms retain part of the resilience benefit. The result is
not autarky. It is a global economy that continues to optimize ordinary goods
for efficiency while optimizing strategic inputs for survivability.

### Passage B: Capital Formation Before Productivity

The sequencing of the AI boom matters. Financial statements record servers,
power equipment, construction, and fabrication plants as investment long before
national accounts capture the full productivity effect of reorganized work.
Firms must first build capacity, redesign processes, clean data, establish
controls, and learn where automation is reliable. Some projects will never
produce an adequate private return; others are valuable mainly because they
protect market position or access under adverse geopolitical conditions. This
creates a predictable wedge between capital formation and measured
productivity. It also distinguishes an AI investment cycle from proof that AI
is already raising economy-wide efficiency. My forecasts intentionally preserve
that wedge: rapid capability growth and very large capital expenditure can
coexist with only a minority probability that U.S. productivity exceeds the
specified threshold. If productivity accelerates before the complementary
capital stock and organizational changes are visible, the framework has been
too pessimistic about diffusion. If capital spending rises while utilization
and productivity remain weak, the risk shifts toward retrenchment rather than
an indefinitely expanding boom.
